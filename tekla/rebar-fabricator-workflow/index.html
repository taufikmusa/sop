You receive the design, you do not author it. Two decisions govern the job: whose concrete you reinforce, and how you will know what changed when they reissue.

B Short Answer
Short answer

A standalone rebar shop receives models rather than authoring them.

The first decision is whether you model the concrete or work against theirs.

  1. Insert the engineer model as a reference, then set change detection.
  2. Decide whether you need concrete parts to hang bars on.
  3. Put your bending shapes into the Rebar shape catalog.
  4. Set your own rebar prefix and start number.
  5. Run numbering, then produce the bending schedules.
  6. On the File menu, click Export, then BVBS.

Every revision from the engineer is change detection first, not remodelling.

Issue Summary

A standalone rebar fabricator is in a different position from everyone else in this set. You did not design the structure, you will not build it, and you rarely own the model. Somebody sends you a design, and you turn it into bar that is cut, bent, bundled and delivered on a date.

That changes what Tekla Structures is for. It is not where your design lives. It is the place where somebody else design becomes your production data, carrying your marks, matched to your bending machinery.

Two questions decide how the whole job runs, and both are answered before any bar is modelled. Do you model the concrete, or work against theirs? And how will you know what changed when they reissue?

This page is the business, not the technique

For which method to use when creating bars — components, rebar sets, shape catalog or manual — the rebar detailing page in this vault covers that in order of preference. This page assumes you know how to make a bar and asks what a fabrication shop does around it.

The First Decision: Whose Concrete

Reinforcement has to attach to something. Reference model objects are not Tekla Structures parts, so bars cannot be attached to them the way they attach to a native concrete part. That leaves three routes, and shops pick different ones for good reasons.

Route What you do What it costs and buys
Model the concrete yourselfRecreate the concrete parts you need to reinforce, using the reference model as the guide, then reinforce them natively.Costs modelling time. Buys adaptive reinforcement: components and rebar sets follow the concrete when it changes, and cast unit or pour grouping works properly.
Convert the reference objectsConvert IFC objects into native Tekla Structures objects, by direct conversion at insert or through conversion change management for selected objects.Costs checking, because converted geometry is only as good as what was exported. Buys native parts without modelling them twice.
Work to the reference visuallyKeep the model as a reference and model bars positioned against it, without native concrete.Costs adaptivity, which is the expensive part. Bars will not follow a section change on their own.

There is no single right answer, but there is a wrong reason. Choosing the third route because it is quickest on day one, on a job you know will be revised four times, converts a modelling saving into a rework bill.

Before You Start

SOURCE

Establish what you are being given

A model, a set of drawings, or a schedule someone else produced are three very different starting points. Ask which, in what format, and how often it will be reissued.

SCHEMA

Ask for a usable IFC

IFC 2X3, IFC 4 and IFC 4.3 are all supported on the sending side. Ask which you are getting, and ask for the property sets you need rather than hoping.

SHAPES

Your machinery decides your shapes

The Rebar shape catalog holds predefined shapes, and Rebar shape manager is where you define your own bending shapes. Your code and your benders decide what belongs there, not the designer.

MARKS

Decide whose marks govern

The engineer bar marks and your production marks are not automatically the same thing. Settle which appears on the schedule, the label and the delivery note before the first issue.

REVISIONS

Assume it will change

Design reinforcement changes more than almost anything else on a job. Set up change detection at the start, when it costs nothing, rather than after the second reissue.

OUTPUT

Confirm what your benders read

BVBS 2.0 from 2000 is the base version. Tekla Structures from 2021 onwards also supports BVBS 3.0 from 2018, which adds coupler and thread data. Know which your machines expect.

The Workflow, Stage by Stage

  1. 1

    Insert what you were sent

    On the File menu, select Import > Insert reference model, and place the engineer model. Check the alignment against a known grid before trusting any position taken from it.

  2. 2

    Set up change detection immediately

    Do not wait for the second issue. Configure a comparison set, which tells you whether Tekla Structures considers a change in a property a change or not, so that when a revision lands you compare rather than re-read.

  3. 3

    Decide the concrete route

    Model the concrete, convert the reference objects, or work visually against the reference, using the table above. Write the decision down, because the next person to open the model needs to know which one they are in.

  4. 4

    Load your shapes

    Make sure the bending shapes your shop actually produces are available. Use the Rebar shape catalog for predefined shapes, and define the rest in Rebar shape manager.

    Put the finished catalog in the firm folder. A shape library rebuilt per job is a shape library that drifts between jobs.

  5. 5

    Reinforce

    Use reinforcement components where they fit, because they are attached to a concrete part and update automatically when it changes, then rebar sets for the rest. The detailing page covers the order of preference in full.

  6. 6

    Set your numbering

    A reinforcement position number consists of a prefix, a separator and a running number. Set the Prefix and Start number in the reinforcing bar properties so bars carry your series, not a default.

    The advanced option XS_REBAR_POSITION_NUMBER_FORMAT_STRING controls how the result is formatted, and XS_REBAR_GEOMETRY_TYPE_IN_NUMBERING specifies how bar geometry is taken into account when bars are compared with each other.

  7. 7

    Consider sequence numbers for the fixers

    Reinforcement sequence numbering assigns running numbers within each cast unit or pour unit, indicating the installation order of bars. It is an optional identifier used in addition to the regular position number REBAR_POS, which remains the basis for identifying identical reinforcement.

    Set it up on the Reinforcement sequence numbering tab of the Numbering Setup dialog, reached from Drawings & reports > Numbering settings > Numbering settings. The Start with list offers Number and Letter, and per-unit start values come from the user-defined attributes RBR_SEQ_START_NO_R for bars, RBR_SEQ_START_NO_M for meshes and RBR_SEQ_START_NO_S for strands.

  8. 8

    Produce the schedules

    Build the bending schedules and pull-out pictures as templates in Template Editor, controlling what information is shown. Reinforcement settings for drawings come from rebar_config.inp in the system folder defined by XS_SYSTEM, the firm folder or the project folder.

  9. 9

    Export to the benders

    On the File menu, click Export > BVBS. The output is a text file in ASCII format with the extension .abs, carrying bent bars, bar groups, meshes and hooks.

  10. 10

    Plan the delivery

    Where you are responsible for getting bar to site in a usable order, Lotting groups assemblies for transport by evaluating what a transfer vehicle can carry, and Sequencer creates sequences and assigns incremental numbers to parts. Used together, each load can be built around the erection sequence rather than around what happened to be finished.

Change Management Is the Job

For a fabrication shop the difference between profit and loss is usually not how fast the first issue was detailed. It is how cheaply the fourth revision was absorbed.

Change detection compares two versions of the same reference model and lists all deleted, changed, new and not changed objects, with the property details limited to the rules in your comparison set. Changed rows that fall within your defined tolerances are highlighted in light yellow, which separates a real change from a coordinate that moved by a millimetre.

  1. 1

    Keep the issued versions

    You cannot compare against something you did not keep. Archive every reference model issue you were sent, named by date and revision.

  2. 2

    Define what counts as a change

    Build the comparison set around what actually affects your bar: geometry and the properties you schedule from. A comparison set that flags everything is as useless as one that flags nothing.

  3. 3

    Compare before you look

    When a revision arrives, run change detection first. Reading a whole model again to find three moved walls is the habit that makes revisions expensive.

  4. 4

    Decide what is already cut

    Bar that has been produced against an earlier issue is a commercial matter, not a modelling one. Knowing precisely which marks are affected, from the comparison, is what turns that conversation into a number.

    Your marks are your evidence

    If your position numbers are stable and traceable to an issue, you can say exactly what was cut against what. If they move every time you renumber, you cannot. Numbering discipline is a commercial control on this kind of work, not just a tidiness habit.
