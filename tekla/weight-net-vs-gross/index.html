Net, Gross or Weight: Which One to Use

Tekla Structures reports three different weights for the same part, and they are meant to answer three different questions. What each one measures, why net can come out heavier than gross, and which one belongs in which report.

A Question

“How is weight calculated in Tekla Structures, and which one should I use? Nett, gross or others?”

Also asked as

  • What is the difference between WEIGHT, WEIGHT_NET and WEIGHT_GROSS in a report?

  • Which weight should I use for buying material and which one for erection?
  • My report weight does not match the weight our fabricator calculated by hand.
  • Why is the tube weight in my report lower than the weight in the steel manual?

B Short Answer
Short answer

Every weight in Tekla Structures is volume multiplied by material density. The three attributes differ only in which volume they use.

  1. WEIGHT_GROSS uses the volume before cuts, so it is the material you buy.
  2. WEIGHT_NET uses the modelled volume after cuts, so it is the fabricated part.
  3. WEIGHT follows the gross value for catalog profiles.

Use gross weight for purchasing and estimating.

Use net weight for shipping, lifting and erection.

Two things to expect: bolt holes are never deducted, and rolled profiles are modelled with square corners.

That second one makes net weight read slightly high on an uncut beam.

Issue Summary

There is no single weight of a part in Tekla Structures. There is one formula — volume multiplied by the density of the material — applied to three different volumes, and the attribute you put in the report decides which volume you get.

Tekla User Assistance defines those volumes plainly. Gross volume is the object volume and does not take cuts into account. Net volume is the object volume and does take cuts into account. Gross weight is therefore the total weight of material needed to fabricate the part, and net weight is the weight of what is left after fabrication.

Which one is right depends entirely on the question the report is answering. Purchasing pays for the steel before it is cut. The crane lifts what is left after it is cut. Those are two different numbers, and both of them are correct.

The Attributes You Can Use

BUYING

WEIGHT_GROSS

The total weight of material needed to fabricate the part. For a catalog profile it comes from the part length, the cross section area in the profile catalog, and the material density. For a folded or contour plate it comes from the plate overall height, overall length and density.

SHIPPING

WEIGHT_NET

The weight of the fabricated part, assembly or cast unit, calculated from the part volume with cuts taken into account. For an assembly it returns the sum of the part weights. For objects that are neither parts nor bolts it returns zero.

DEFAULT

WEIGHT

The one most standard reports already use. For a part whose cross section is in the profile catalog it matches WEIGHT_GROSS. For other profiles it is the net weight from the profile volume, where fittings, cuts, weld preparations and part additions all affect the volume.

REBAR

WEIGHT_TOTAL

For reinforcement, WEIGHT gives the weight of one bar only. WEIGHT_TOTAL gives all the bars in the group. Getting these two mixed up is one of the quieter ways a rebar schedule ends up wrong.

CATALOG

PROFILE_WEIGHT

The tabulated weight from the profile catalog rather than a calculation from the modelled solid. Tekla User Assistance recommends swapping WEIGHT for this one when your figures have to agree with published steel manual values.

BILLING

CUSTOM.BILLINGWEIGHT

A custom attribute from the Billing Weight Attribute extension, for contour plates and beam plates. It sits deliberately between the other two, so the order becomes gross weight, then billing weight, then net weight.

How the Number Is Actually Produced

  1. 1

    A volume is taken

    For a profile with a cross section in the catalog, the gross volume is the catalog cross section area multiplied by LENGTH. The net volume is the volume of the solid as actually modelled, with cuts and fittings applied.

  2. 2

    A density is chosen

    This part surprises people. If the profile has cross sections defined in the profile catalog, Tekla Structures uses the Profile density property in the material catalog. If it does not, it uses the Plate density property instead.

    Two grades that look identical in the model can therefore report different weights if one of those two catalog fields was edited and the other was not.

  3. 3

    The two are multiplied

    Weight is volume multiplied by density, and nothing else. The worked example in Tekla User Assistance uses steel at 0.00000785 kg per cubic millimetre, which is the familiar 7850 kg per cubic metre.

    Rolled profile, uncut, from Tekla User Assistance:

  Gross volume = cross section area x LENGTH = 5 550 000 mm3
  Gross weight = 5 550 000 x 0.00000785     = 43.56 kg

  Net volume   = modelled solid             = 5 600 000 mm3
  Net weight   = 5 600 000 x 0.00000785     = 43.96 kg

Plate PL50 1200x1200, 400x400 chamfer and one 800 dia hole:

  Gross weight = 1.200 x 1.200 x 0.050 x 7850.00 = 565.20 kg
  Cuts removed                                   = 228.69 kg
  Net weight                                     = 336.51 kg
  4. 4

    Check it yourself on a real part

    Use the Inquire command on a part, or run a report, and read the volume and weight values side by side. Doing this on one known beam and one known plate is the fastest way to settle an argument about which number the report is showing.

Net heavier than gross is not a mistake

Look at the first example above: the uncut beam reports 43.96 kg net against 43.56 kg gross. Net is the larger number, which reads like nonsense until you know why. Gross comes from the catalog cross section area, which includes the root fillets and corner roundings of the rolled section. The modelled solid is built with straight angles and no roundings, so it contains slightly more steel than the real profile does. On an uncut member that difference is all you are seeing. Add cuts and net drops below gross, as expected.

Traps Worth Knowing Before You Publish a Report

HOLES

Bolt holes are never deducted

Tekla User Assistance states it directly: bolt holes are not deducted from the weight of the part. Not from gross, and not from net. If your billing depends on hole deductions, that is what the Billing Weight Attribute extension exists for.

ROUNDINGS

Net weight is not the true fabricated weight

The documentation is unusually blunt here. Because the cross section is built with straight angles and no corner roundings, the net weight is not the actual weight of the fabricated part unless the higher accuracy option is switched on.

TUBES

Hollow sections in imperial units

HSS weights come out around seven per cent below the values a fabricator gets from the AISC tables, because Tekla Structures multiplies the modelled volume by the profile density instead of using weight per unit length.

SURFACE

Surface treatment rides along

For a part that has surface treatment, WEIGHT shows the part and the treatment added together. Worth remembering before you go hunting for the missing kilograms elsewhere.

PARAMETRIC

Parametric profiles collapse the difference

A profile with no cross section data in the catalog uses the modelled volume for everything. Volume, gross volume and net volume become the same figure, so all three weights report the same number and comparing them tells you nothing.

ZERO

Zeros in a mixed report

WEIGHT_NET returns zero for objects that are neither parts nor bolts. A report that mixes object types can therefore total up short without showing an obvious error anywhere.

If You Need a Closer Net Weight

There are two documented routes, and the second one is the one to reach for first.

ROUTE 1

Exact part presentation

Setting XS_SOLID_USE_HIGHER_ACCURACY to TRUE in the options.ini file in the model folder brings part volume, area and net weight closer to real manufactured values. The default is FALSE. It is powerful and it is expensive, so read the warning below before you switch it on.

ROUTE 2

A correction factor in the template

Tekla User Assistance documents correcting the net weight in the report template instead, by multiplying it by the catalog cross section area divided by the modelled cross section area. No model-wide setting, no performance cost.

GetValue("WEIGHT_NET") * (GetValue("PROFILE.CROSS_SECTION_AREA")
                          / GetValue("AREA_PROJECTION_YZ_NET"))

Apply that correction only where it is valid: steel parts that are not items, not polybeams, with straight segments, and either hot rolled or rectangular in shape. Outside those limits the factor does not hold.

What the higher accuracy option costs you

Tekla User Assistance warns against activating XS_SOLID_USE_HIGHER_ACCURACY when you create NC or DSTV files, because it can lead to inaccuracies in the exported data. It also warns against it when you create single part or assembly drawings, because it can lead to unnecessary dimension lines. On top of that it increases the number of faces in solid objects, which slows Tekla Structures down. A more accurate weight column is rarely worth a compromised NC file, so treat this as a deliberate decision for the whole model rather than a quick fix for one report.

When the Fabricator Disagrees With Your Report

This comes up most often with hollow sections in an imperial environment. Tekla Structures multiplies the modelled volume by the profile density from the material catalog. The fabricator multiplies the AISC weight per unit length by the member length. The second method gives the higher figure, and the gap runs at roughly seven per cent.

Neither side is doing arithmetic wrong. They are answering with different source data, and Tekla User Assistance offers two documented ways to line them up.

TEMPLATE FIX

Swap the attribute

In the report template, replace GetValue("WEIGHT") with GetValue("PROFILE_WEIGHT") so the report returns the tabulated catalog value.

CATALOG FIX

Adjust the profile density

For rectangular tube materials, change the Profile density from 490.06 lbf per cubic foot to 526.10 lbf per cubic foot in the material catalog, which brings the calculation into line with the tabulated values.

Change the template before you change the catalog

Both fixes are documented, but they are not equal in blast radius. Editing the profile density in the material catalog changes every weight for that material everywhere in the model, including reports and drawings you were happy with. Swapping the attribute in one report template changes exactly one report. Start there, agree the numbers, and only move to the catalog if the whole company needs the change and the change is applied to a shared catalog on purpose.
